# Launch video marketing

Deciding *what happens when* and *why* in a launch video — the script and
structure that a video-authoring skill then implements. A beautifully
animated video with the wrong structure still fails; use this before writing
scene code, not after.

## The structural formula

Every effective launch video follows some variant of this arc. Not every
beat needs its own scene — short cuts (under 20s) compress several beats
into one; long cuts (60s+) can give each its own scene:

1. **Hook (0–3s).** A claim, question, or visual that stops the scroll.
   Never open with a logo or company name — nobody has decided to care yet.
   Open with the *problem/tension* the product resolves, stated in the
   viewer's language, not yours. ("Markets never sleep" beats "Introducing
   Ecelon" as an opener.)
2. **Context (3–8s).** One sentence establishing why the hook matters *now*
   or *to this viewer*. Keep it to a single idea — this is not the place for
   a feature list.
3. **Value / demonstration (the bulk of the runtime).** Show, don't just
   claim. Prioritize by resonance, not by internal importance — the feature
   your team is proudest of and the feature that best sells the hook are
   often different; lead with the latter. If showing multiple capabilities,
   order them **weakest-to-strongest or strongest-first-then-supporting**,
   never randomly — closing on a mid-tier feature undersells the finish.
4. **Proof (optional but high-leverage).** Numbers, real users/quotes, a
   before/after — anything that isn't just the company's own claim about
   itself. Skepticism is the default state of any viewer watching an ad;
   proof beats is what overcomes it. Place it *after* the value is
   understood, not before — proof is meaningless out of context.
5. **Brand moment.** This is where the logo/name lands — after value has
   been established, not before. A viewer who now understands what the
   product does is finally ready to attach a name to it.
6. **CTA (final 2–4s).** One action, stated plainly, with zero ambiguity
   about what happens next ("Get early access," "Try it free," not a vague
   "Learn more" unless that's genuinely the only next step available).

## The first three seconds are a separate discipline

Across every platform, the majority of drop-off happens in the first 3
seconds — before the algorithm even knows to keep serving the video, and
before a human has decided to keep watching.

- **No preamble.** No logo-then-tagline slow build, no "in today's market..."
  throat-clearing. Cut straight to the sharpest visual or claim.
- **Pattern interrupt beats a pretty shot.** Motion, a surprising number, a
  direct question, or an unexpected visual outperforms a beautiful-but-inert
  establishing shot for stopping a scroll.
- **Assume sound-off.** Design the hook to work as pure visual + on-screen
  text; treat audio/voiceover as reinforcement, not the delivery mechanism,
  for at least the opening beat.
- **Curiosity gap, not full reveal.** The hook should create a question the
  rest of the video answers ("Five specialists. Zero downtime." implies
  "what specialists, doing what?") rather than answering everything upfront
  and leaving nothing to watch for.

## Messaging hierarchy — one core message per video

A launch video that tries to communicate everything communicates nothing.
Before scripting:

- Pick **one** primary message (the single sentence a viewer should be able
  to repeat back five minutes later). Every other beat supports it or gets cut.
- **Benefit before feature.** "Now neither does your portfolio" (benefit) is
  what a viewer holds onto — "five autonomous trading agents" (feature) is
  what the *next* beat proves it with. Lead each section with the benefit,
  follow with the feature/mechanism that makes it credible.
- **Specificity beats superlatives.** "+44% annual ROI, 69% win rate" is
  more persuasive than "the best trading platform" — concrete, checkable
  numbers read as more credible than adjectives precisely because they're
  falsifiable.
- **Cut anything that's true but not persuasive.** Internal roadmap details,
  technical architecture, minor features — save it for documentation. A
  launch video's job is to move someone from unaware to curious-enough-to-click,
  not to be comprehensive.

## Platform conventions

Format and pacing are not portable across platforms — a video optimized for
one under-performs on another if reused unmodified (see `remotion`'s
`reference/multi-format.md` for the technical side of exporting variants):

| Platform | Aspect | Typical length | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| YouTube (launch trailer / ad) | 16:9 | 30–90s | Only platform where a slower, more cinematic opening 2–3s is survivable; still don't waste it. Sound-on is a safer assumption here than social feeds. |
| LinkedIn | 1:1 or 4:5 | 15–45s | Feed-scroll, sound-off by default, professional register — proof/numbers land especially well here. Captions mandatory. |
| Instagram/TikTok/Shorts (Reels) | 9:16 | 6–30s | Fastest drop-off curve of any format — hook must land in the first 1–2s. Native-feeling (less "produced," more direct) often outperforms polished. Captions mandatory, big and high-contrast. |
| X/Twitter | 16:9 or 1:1 | 15–45s | Autoplay muted in-feed — same sound-off rule as LinkedIn/TikTok. |
| Website hero | 16:9 or 21:9 | 15–40s, often looping | Usually muted-autoplay loop — needs to read cleanly with no audio and no clear "end," or should loop seamlessly. |

Rule of thumb when producing one hero asset and cutting variants: the
**16:9 hero can be the longest and most complete arc**; social cuts should
be re-edited (not just cropped) to open on the strongest 2 seconds of the
hero cut, drop the slowest-paced scene entirely, and compress the CTA.

## Retention psychology beyond the hook

- **Deliver a small payoff every 3–5 seconds.** A new visual, a new number,
  a scene change — viewers re-decide to keep watching continuously, not just
  once at the start. A static or repetitive stretch is where drop-off spikes.
- **Escalate, don't plateau.** Each proof point or feature shown should feel
  like a step up from the last (more impressive number, bigger claim,
  broader capability) — flat or repetitive beats (three features shown with
  identical pacing and identical emphasis) read as filler even if each is
  individually true and relevant.
- **Callback the hook at the end.** Echoing the opening line or visual right
  before/at the CTA ("your agents are already working" answering "markets
  never sleep") gives the video a sense of closure and reinforces the one
  message you picked.

## CTA discipline

- **One CTA, not a menu.** "Sign up, follow us, check out our blog, and DM
  us" is zero CTAs in practice — pick the single next action that matters
  most for this video's goal.
- **Make the action concrete and low-friction-sounding.** "Get early access"
  > "Learn more" > "Check it out." Specificity implies the path is short.
- **Show the CTA, don't just say it**, when the format allows — a button/UI
  element materializing (as in an app or website) is more persuasive than
  text alone because it previews the actual next step.

## Working with a video-authoring skill

Once the structure above is decided, hand off: "scene 1 = hook (X seconds,
says Y), scene 2 = ..." to the `remotion` skill (or equivalent) for
implementation, and to `motion-design` for how each beat should move and
look. This skill's output is the *script and shot list*, not the code.
